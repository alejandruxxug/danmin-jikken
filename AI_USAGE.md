# AI usage

> Running log, written as the project happens so it matches the defense. Rows are in date order; each one is a real mistake found in AI-written code or advice.

## Tools
- **Claude Code** (Anthropic), run in several sessions. The work is split between three project agents (defined in `.claude/agents/`): **`art`** on **Claude Opus** (Aseprite sprites, Figma views, art reviews), **`builder`** on **Claude Sonnet** (Go code: the model, the simulation, the game, tests) and **`tester`** on **Claude Sonnet** (runs the checks and drives the game through the agent backdoor; reports bugs, never fixes them). The main session coordinates them. Design docs, the `hospital` model, the demo, the tests and the game were all written with this setup.
- Skills used by the agents: the pixel-game-studio skill ([ClaudeWD40](https://github.com/alejandruxxug/ClaudeWD40)), made for this game (see below).
- The design, the story, the scope and every approval of a screen are Alejo's decisions (the visual approval gate: nothing is coded before he approves its Figma view). The AI proposed and built; the rows below are the mistakes found along the way.

## The pixel-game-studio skill
A Claude Code skill Alejo and Claude made on 2026-10-14 from the way this game was built, so the same workflow can be reused on other 2D games. It lives in his skills repo: [`skills/pixel-game-studio`](https://github.com/alejandruxxug/ClaudeWD40/tree/main/skills/pixel-game-studio) (commit `c2b0f7d`).

| Part | What it does |
|---|---|
| `SKILL.md` | Makes the main Claude session the **producer**: it talks to the student, writes decisions down, hands work to the agents and relays between them. It does very little drawing or coding itself |
| The three lanes | `art` (Opus: Aseprite sprites, Figma views), `builder` (Sonnet: game code, tests), `tester` (Sonnet: drives the game, reports, never fixes). Templates in `references/agents/`, the same agents this repo has in `.claude/agents/` |
| The approval gate | No screen is coded before its Figma view is approved. Every decision goes into the design log in the student's own words |
| The visual-issue loop | `tester` spots something that looks wrong → `art` confirms it and fixes only that → `builder` if the cause is code → `tester` re-checks |
| `references/agent-backdoor.md` | How to give the game a test mode (`-agent -scene <id>`, scripted input, screenshots, state dumps), like this game's backdoor |
| `references/prompts.md` | Prompt templates for each agent: the student's words, what's approved, exact paths, which files the agent may touch, the checks and what to report |
| `references/sound.md` | Rules for finding sound legally (licences, credits) and letting the student choose by ear |

Why it matters here: it writes down the method used for the GUI and the night (who decides, who draws, who codes, who tests), so the workflow is something Alejo can explain, not something hidden in old chats. The game's credits name it ("Made with: the pixel-game-studio skill").

## Decisive prompts
The three prompts that really shaped the game, out of about 190. The quotes are exactly what I typed, typos included.

1. **Turning the homework into a game.** The assignment is a hospital in Go with OOP, and the professor likes worked-up mini games, so on day one I wrote: *"The teacher is calling for an oop in go but i can haggle the programming lang if it includes the assginment tasks"*. A bit later came the line that is basically the whole game: *"separate the day and night since day is full trabajo and night is full visual novel"* (trabajo = the assignment). The day is the assignment, playable: every click, stamp and query calls the real `hospital` package. The night is the visual novel and it's worth 0 points, it's just for me. That split is also why `hospital/` could be frozen after M1 and the GUI only uses `Hospital`'s methods, like the brief asks.

2. **The day is false.** While reading the first story draft I said: *"the time we're really awake would be at night. And then uh, the day would be like a, like a false."* That changed everything. The game opens on a gurney, you close your eyes and wake up in a white day. The day has a CRT on it because you're kind of watching it through a screen, the strobe throws you into the night, and on day 3 the last patient at the window is you. Before that prompt we just had a day and a night. After it, they had a reason.

3. **How I worked with the agents.** *"tell the sonnet agent if somehting looks fishy and out of place tell opus, confirm it and opus fixes that exact thing."* That became the method: Opus draws in Aseprite and mocks the screens in Figma, Sonnet builds and tests, nothing gets coded until I approve the screen, and every milestone starts with fresh agents. I decide how it looks and feels, the AI builds it, and the tester catches what I'd miss. Most of the mistakes in the table above were caught that way. Later we turned the method into the pixel-game-studio skill (above).

## What I learned
### OOP in Go
Go has no classes and no inheritance, and that was the first thing I had to get over.
- **Composition, not inheritance.** `Doctor`, `Nurse`, `Orderly` and `Patient` all embed `Person` by value. A doctor isn't a "subclass" of a person, it *has* a person inside, and Go promotes `ID()` and `Name()` so it reads the same.
- **Interfaces are implicit.** `Attender` is just three methods (`ID`, `Name`, `Attend`). Nobody writes "implements"; if the type has the methods, it is one. The hospital keeps its staff in a `[]Attender` and sends whoever is next without knowing if it's a doctor or a nurse. That's why adding the Nurse in M1 didn't touch the dispatch code at all. The `var _ Attender = (*Nurse)(nil)` lines make the compiler check it for me.
- **Encapsulation is per package.** Lowercase fields can't be touched from outside `hospital/`, so the game can only go through methods. That's what let me freeze the model after M1 and build the whole GUI on top without changing it, like the brief asks.
- **Typed constants.** `PatientState` with `iota` and a `String()` method instead of loose strings, so a typo is a compile error, not a bug.
- **Errors are values.** No exceptions and no `panic` for normal stuff: `ErrNoRoomAvailable` and friends, wrapped with `%w` to say which patient, and checked with `errors.Is`. "No room" is just something that happens in a hospital, not a crash.
- **Goroutines need locks.** Every patient is a goroutine that falls asleep on its own, so the hospital has a mutex and the tests run with `-race`. The AI's first version read the episode history without a lock, which was fine with one goroutine and a data race with many (it's in the table). That one taught me the race detector isn't optional.

### Game design
- **I can picture my ideas now.** The Forever ending I described beat by beat (the rope, the railing breaking, the blood on the camera) came out *literally* how I imagined it. Seeing it in my head and then on screen is the skill I'm proudest of.
- **References are a tool.** Almost every decision was "like X, but…": FNAF 2 but at a reception, DDLC's meta stuff, Slay the Princess at night. Mixing things you know is how you get something new.
- **Playtesting beats theory.** My best changes came after playing: the strobe leaking the night, the endings not being cinematic enough, not knowing the rules on day 1 (that became the tutorial).
- **Design moments, not features.** The music starting quiet and dropping right on the title, the pause on the face before the stun. Timing is what makes people feel something.
- **Turn the constraint into the game.** "A hospital in Go with OOP" became the day shift you actually play, instead of a box I had to tick.
- **Cutting is design too.** The scope board and going from more endings down to four (Sleep, Wake, Forever, 3:17).

### Working with AI
- **I direct, it builds.** I decided how it looks and feels, the AI wrote most of the code, and nothing got coded until I approved the screen. Splitting it into agents (Opus draws, Sonnet builds and tests) worked like running a tiny studio, and I turned that into the pixel-game-studio skill.
- **The AI is confidently wrong sometimes.** Everything in the table above looked fine until a test, a screenshot or me playing it said otherwise. You need a way to check, not just trust.
- **Understanding is still on me.** The AI writing a line doesn't mean I can explain it. Going through the code for the defense is part of the project, not extra.

### For me
- **Finishing.** Builds for three systems, a trailer, and the class loved it. Lots of people start games, this one is done.
- **Pacing myself.** The "DONE by morning" 4 a.m. sprints worked this time, but next time I want to plan it better.
